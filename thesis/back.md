## Embedded Systems 

### Hardware 



The CPU (Central Processing Unit) is the processor that executes
instructions. An MCU (microcontroller) combines a CPU with
memory and peripherals on a single chip for a specific embedded task.
A SoC (System on Chip) integrates a CPU and other system
components into one chip and may support a broader range of system
functions than an MCU, which is optimized for focused control tasks.
A SoM (System on Module) is a small board built around an SoC,
with supporting components such as RAM, storage, and power management.
\cite{jlbpcb2026system} In short, the CPU is the processing component 
inside an MCU or SoC, and a SoM packages an SoC and supporting hardware 
as a module. 

On a SoM, RAM (Random Access Memory) provides temporary working 
space for the processor and loses its contents when power is removed. 
Flash memory stores data persistently; it may be mounted directly on the 
SoM, for example as eMMC, or provided by a removable SD  (Secure Digital)
card. The SD card is typically inserted into a slot on the carrier board, 
which connects it to the SoM. \cite{jimhandy2023flash} Thus, RAM supports 
active program execution, while flash stores software and data between 
power cycles. These items are detailed in Figure \ref{fig:PCB}

communication, allowing devices to talk to each other using two wires. \\

"edge" of the network) close to where the data is collected, rather than 
sending it to a centralized cloud or data center. \\

\textbf{IoT} - Internet of Things - A network of physical objects ("things") 
embedded with sensors, software, and processing units (like MCUs or SoCs) 
that connect and exchange data with other devices and systems over the

## Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning 

\begingroup
\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}
\begin{singlespace}

\textbf{AI} - Artificial Intelligence - The broader concept of machines 
being able to carry out tasks in a way that mimics human intelligence, 
reasoning, or problem-solving. \\

\textbf{ML} - Machine Learning - A subset of AI focused on building systems 
that learn from data, identify patterns, and make decisions with minimal 
human intervention. \\ 

\textbf{DL} - Deep Learning - A specialized subfield of ML that utilizes 
multi-layered artificial neural networks to solve complex problems \\

\textbf{Algorithm} - A set of step-by-step mathematical rules or 
instructions given to an AI system to help it learn from data 
or solve a specific problem. \\

\textbf{Training Data} - The initial dataset used to teach an ML model 
how to find patterns, make predictions, or perform a specific task. \\

\textbf{Supervised Learning} - A type of ML where the model is trained 
on labeled data. \\

\textbf{Unsupervised Learning} - A type of ML where the model analyzes 
unlabeled data to discover hidden patterns or groupings on its own. \\ 

\textbf{Model} - The output generated by an ML algorithm after training 
on a dataset. It represents what the system has learned and is used to 
make predictions on new data. \\

\textbf{Neural Network} - A computing system inspired by the biological 
neural networks of the human brain, consisting of interconnected nodes. \\

\textbf{Backpropagation} - The foundational algorithm used to train neural 
networks by calculating the error at the output layer and passing it 
backward through the network to adjust the connection weights and improve 
accuracy. \\

\textbf{LLM} - Large Language Model - A deep learning model trained on 
massive amounts of text data, capable of understanding, generating, and 
translating human language. \\

\textbf{Computer Vision} - A field of AI that trains computers to interpret 
and understand the visual world. \\

\textbf{Overfitting} - A common mistake where a model learns the training 
data too well, memorizing the noise and details instead of the general 
patterns, making it perform poorly on new data. \\

\textbf{Underfitting} - A scenario where a model is too simple to capture 
the underlying patterns in the data, resulting in poor performance on both 
the training and new data. \\

\textbf{Bias} - Systematic errors in an AI system caused by flawed 
assumptions in the ML process or unrepresentative training data. \\ 

\textbf{Inference} - The process of using a trained ML model to make 
predictions or decisions on new, unseen real-world data. \\

\textbf{FQT} - Fully Quantized Training - A training method where both the 
forward and backward passes of a neural network are calculated using low-bit 
mathematical formats (like 8-bit integers instead of 32-bit floating points) 
to drastically reduce memory usage and accelerate training. \\

\textbf{Edge Computing} - The practice of running machine learning models 
and processing data locally on physical hardware devices (like sensors or 
microcontrollers) rather than relying on a cloud server, enabling instant 
predictions, lower latency, and increased privacy. \\


\end{singlespace}

## Related work

Artificial intelligence and machine learning have become more and more
important in embedded and edge computing applications, but the
workloads usually require significant computational resources. Modern ML
models rely on large memory allocations, extensive matrix operations,
and highly parallel processing to perform training, inference, and
predictions efficiently.

In many cases, the computational requirements are dominated by
CPU/GPU/NPU parallelism, high-bandwidth memory access, and fast clock
rates, which is why these systems are typically associated
with desktop-class or server-class hardware. As discussed in recent
reviews of edge AI, the challenge is not simply running a model, but
doing so within the energy, memory, and latency constraints of the
target platform \cite{patel2026}.

Embedded systems, by contrast, are designed around different priorities.
They are typically optimized for low power consumption, compact size,
deterministic real-time behavior, and reliability in constrained
operating environments. In many industrial and control applications,
the available memory, storage, and thermal budgets are limited, and the
system may run without a full desktop operating system or traditional
persistent storage.

These constraints make embedded controllers well suited for control
loops, sensor acquisition, and lightweight automation, but less natural
for the large-scale training and parallelized compute commonly
associated with AI/ML workloads
\cite{pinto2019demystifying}.

The contrast is important when considering whether AI can be deployed
on a commercial HVAC controller or similar embedded platform.
Traditional machine learning tasks like regression, classification,
and simple inference pipelines may fit within the capabilities of
modern embedded hardware, if the models are lightweight and the
application is bounded by smaller data rates.

Anomaly detection, predictive maintenance, classification, and simple
forecasting can be implemented on edge devices when the model is
carefully selected and the compute workload is matched to the hardware.
In these cases, the goal is not to replace a high-end workstation, but
to bring useful intelligence as close as possible to the physical
system.

In many cases, each HVAC installation is designed around the specific
requirements of a given building, occupant use pattern, and thermal
load. A school, a church, and a commercial office may share similar
building footprints, but their daily operating schedules, occupancy
patterns, and heating or cooling demands can differ substantially.
This variability makes it difficult to apply a single universal model
or control strategy across all environments, even when the equipment is
similar in form and function.

At the same time, the feasibility of AI on embedded systems depends
heavily on the specific workload. Training large deep learning models,
running high-dimensional computer vision pipelines, or handling speech
and language models in real time can quickly exceed the memory,
thermal, and latency budgets of low-power embedded hardware.

In many industrial settings, the embedded controller is expected to
execute real-time control actions while maintaining communication,
safety, and reliability requirements. This means that while embedded
AI is feasible for some tasks, it remains constrained by model
complexity, data volume, processing time, and the need to maintain
deterministic system behavior.

The modern embedded controller sits in a middle ground between
traditional embedded control and full-scale AI acceleration.
As hardware platforms continue to improve—through the inclusion of
NPUs, GPUs, more memory, faster ARM cores, and richer software
stacks, the range of feasible edge AI applications is growing. The
central question is no longer whether AI is possible on embedded
systems, but rather which classes of ML algorithms are practical,
efficient, and safe for the target application under real-world
constraints.

### Foundational Literature Reviews 

Deep Learning and Machine Learning and AI in general have become foundational
parts of technology and society as a whole.  This section is reviewing a 
few works where the authors discuss the training of AI models is 
moving closer to the 'edge' of netowrked embeded systems also called 
the Internet of Things (IoT). More recently, the concept of exploiting the 
mobile and embedded computing resources for ML/DL model training has gained 
attention, as such capabilities allow (1) the training of models via local 
data without the need to share data over wireless links, thus enabling 
privacy-preserving computation by design, (2) model personalization and 
environment adaptation, and (3) deployment of accurate models in remote 
and hardly accessible locations without stable internet connectivity.
\cite{morgese2024ondevice}

Additional Edge AI research also looks to minimize the amounts of memory 
needed for training models on a device.  An added benefit to edge training
is the security of not having to transfer sensitive information over the 
cloud.  Privacy in this way is protected.  Full backpropagation is a 
memory and computation resource intense operation.  Ji Lin and team have 
created a way using sparse updating and special gradient calibration to 
reduce the memory footprint and implement lightweight trainign with similar
accuracy to normal training.  \cite{lin2022ondevice} This enables IoT 
devices to continuously adapt to new data for learning while also 
performing inference.  

The Heydari paper focuses on using TinyML for inference on the
embedded device and not on training. There are multiple uses for 
on-device inference and near real-time applicability, including for 
use in IoT with autonomous vehicles. The latencey requirements for 
such uses make TinyML a viable solution in these cases. 
\cite{heydari2023tinyml}


### On-Device Learning - Memory Efficient methods

There are many examples and research around this and similar problems
of how embedded devices can successfully run AI and ML on the edge.  
Tiny Machine Learning is a fast-growing research area committed to
democratizing deep learning for all-pervasive microcontrollers (MCUs).
\cite{ren2021tinyol}. This differs in the current research in that
only the inference is performed on the edge. The training part is
performed on larger more powerful machines.

The resulting models from performing only inference and not training
are hard to adapt to new data. TinyOL has an online component that
uses streaming of data to train models.

The use of hybrid infrastructures, where the training phase of learn
models is moved to a more capable cloud computing platform, is becoming
more prevalent. \cite{vita2023ondevice} Communication latencies and
security problems may cause issues with this approach in certain case
where these problems become critical (defense systems, real-time operations).  
In these cases, on device or edge training is ideal. Reservoir computing
is a proposed solution to this problem.

Edge computing is aimed at reducing the cloud workload to process 
device data by means of performing some preprocessing and/or computing 
tasks at the network edge \cite{10.1145/3486674}. While this research is 
related to the topic of AI on the edge, it inherently implies a connection 
to the web and cloud platforms.  Edge AI in this case doesn't necessarly 
mean training and inference on the embedded hardware, but very close 
as to not lose time to network latency. See Figure \ref{fig:EdgeCompute}
